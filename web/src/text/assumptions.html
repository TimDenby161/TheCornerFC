What the models assume

Page last updated 22 October 2026

Every model rests on assumptions: things it takes to be true so that it can work at all. This page lists the ones behind the club ratings, the player ratings, the predicted line-ups and the match predictions. For each one it says what is assumed, why, and how it has been checked. How the numbers are worked out is on How the models work; how the model is doing, and every change made to it, is on The Model.

Each assumption carries one of three labels, so you can see how much weight it bears:

Tested on past matches
Compared with the alternatives by re-running the model on past matches, using only what was known before each one, with the setting chosen on one period and scored on a later one. Useful evidence, but not proof: those seasons had been looked at before.
Checked on live matches
Measured on predictions saved before kick-off, which can't be edited afterwards. The strongest evidence there is, but the record only began on 26 September 2026.
Judgement
A choice that seemed sensible and has not been tested against results, usually because the data can't test it. These are the ones most worth questioning.

Nothing here is betting advice, and betting is left out of this page. The model's predictions are probabilities, not guarantees.

The data

The data we receive is right, and complete enough

Judgement Tested on past matches

Assumes: results, line-ups, player statistics, xG, shots and injury lists from our one data source, API-Football, are correct.

Why: it is the only source we use, and every model is built on it.

Checked: league tables worked out from the results were compared with API-Football's own standings, all 1,129 rows, on 6 October 2026: they agreed everywhere except where API-Football's table was behind its own results. The gaps we know of are listed on How the models work: injury lists in only some leagues, xG for about a third to a half of matches. Anything the source gets wrong, we get wrong.

Only what was known before kick-off

Checked on live matches

Assumes: a prediction uses nothing that happened after the moment it was made.

Why: otherwise a model looks better than it is.

Checked: every prediction is saved before kick-off with the time it was made, and the database refuses any change to it afterwards. Tests on past matches replay every match in kick-off order, so each one sees only the matches before it. One known leak: some past inputs were rebuilt later with today's data, which is why a test on past matches is treated as the best the model could hope for, not what to expect.

xG says more about how well a side played than goals do

Tested on past matches

Assumes: where a match has xG (expected goals, the quality of the chances each side had), it is a better guide to how well each side played than the score.

Why: goals are rare, so one deflection or one save changes a score; the quality of the chances moves far less with luck.

Checked: rating clubs on 70% xG and 30% score beat goals alone in each of 2023/24, 2024/25 and 2025/26 taken separately, and beat xG alone. The score is kept at 30% because xG can't see everything (a finisher who really is better, say).

Shots can stand in for xG where xG is missing

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: where a match has shot counts but no xG (about 40% of matches), an xG worked out from shots, shots on target and shots in the box says something real.

Why: otherwise those matches move the ratings on the score alone.

Checked: chosen on July 2022 to June 2024 and scored on July 2024 to October 2026, it improved the predictions, and it went live on 14 October 2026 in one release with the two changes under new and promoted clubs. That release is being compared with the version before it on live matches, and can be undone if it does worse.

Club strength

One number captures how strong a club is

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: a club's strength can be summed up in one rating (Current Strength), on a scale where 100 points is worth about one goal a game, with attack, defence and a home edge worked out alongside it.

Why: it is the simplest thing that works, and it lets a club be compared with any other, in any competition.

Checked: the whole match model rests on it. Run on nothing but dates, clubs and scores, the ratings alone keep 96% of the model's gain over guessing the usual share of home wins, draws and away wins. The model's live record is on The Model.

Home advantage is about the same for everyone

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: playing at home is worth 0.3 goals for every club in every competition, plus a small edge of each club's own learned from its results, and 0.2 goals more in the Champions League, Europa League and Conference League.

Why: past results put it at about 0.3 goals almost everywhere. A spreadsheet method the model started from gave strong clubs 0.4 to 1.1 goals, which was too much.

Checked: each club's own edge helped a little on past matches, and most clubs' edges are within a few points. A different home advantage for each competition helped a little on past matches but has not been proven, and is being scored on live matches.

A big win says no more than a three-goal win

Tested on past matches

Assumes: a 7–0 counts as 3–0 when ratings are updated.

Why: a side that is already well ahead eases off, or the other side gives up; a freak score shouldn't swing a rating.

Checked: with the cap and smaller steps, the error in predicted goal difference fell from 1.77 to 1.68 goals a match.

Strength changes a little after every match

Tested on past matches

Assumes: after each match a club moves by how much better or worse it did than expected, by a fixed step: larger when the match has xG or shots, since those say more. A club's Baseline Strength, its long-term level over about its last 100 matches, moves more slowly.

Why: clubs do change, but one result is mostly noise.

Checked: the step sizes were chosen on past matches. Different steps for the first matches after the summer, and pulling ratings towards their league's level after it, were tried and didn't help. Using the strength of the starting XI in the update was tried and didn't help either.

New and promoted clubs

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: a club with few matches behind it is less well known, so it moves further after each one (half as far again for its first, close to normal after 120). A club promoted or relegated is moved halfway to the average of the division it joins when it starts playing there. A club seen for the first time starts at a level set for its league.

Why: otherwise a promoted club carries its old rating into a stronger division, and a new club takes months to find its level.

Checked: the first two went live with shots for xG (above) on 14 October 2026 and are being checked on live matches. The starting level for each league is a judgement.

A match today depends more on current form

Tested on past matches

Assumes: for a match today, a club's strength is 60% Current Strength and 40% Baseline Strength; for a match further away it leans more on the baseline, down to 20% current a year or more ahead.

Why: form fades, so the further away a match is, the more the long-term level tells you.

Checked: 60/40 beat every other mix tried in both 2022/23 and 2024 onwards; with ratings as they stood months before a match, a lower current share did best.

Curtain-raisers count for a third

Judgement

Assumes: the Community Shield and the UEFA Super Cup move ratings a third as much as other matches.

Why: clubs treat them as pre-season. A third is the ratio a well-known rating of national teams uses for friendlies.

Checked: not tested: there are only two or three a year.

Player ratings

Being a regular for a strong club is evidence of quality

Tested on past matches

Assumes: a player's Ability starts from the long-term strength of the clubs he has been a regular for, and his stats then move him up or down by up to about 15 points (more at the strongest clubs).

Why: strong clubs choose their players with far more information than we have, and stats depend heavily on the team around a player.

Checked: an earlier version built this way repeated from one season to the next better than the one before it, including for players who changed club. Versions with club strength taken out did no better at predicting players' later seasons and did worse at predicting match results. The current version has not been through that check again.

Counted stats show what a player does, and each position needs its own mix

Judgement

Assumes: counted stats per 90 minutes, such as tackles, passes, key passes, shots on target and goals, compared with other players in the same position, measure a player's contribution. Each position has its own mix; every weight is listed in the player ratings guide, with the model's code.

Why: a centre-back and a striker are asked to do different things.

Checked: the mixes were set by judgement in October 2026, by looking at where well-known players landed, starting from mixes that had been checked against results and against how well each stat repeats from season to season. The final mixes themselves have not been tested against results. A data provider's own match ratings are not used.

A skill's numbers mean a little more in a harder league

Tested on past matches

Assumes: the same player puts up slightly different numbers in different leagues, by a fixed amount for each league and skill. A player's skill scores (finishing, creating, involvement and the rest, 0 to 100) have that amount taken off for the leagues he played in, measured from players who played regular minutes in more than one league.

Why: without it, a lower-league player's numbers were set against the Premier League's as they stood.

Checked: fitted on seasons up to 2024 and tested on 606 later moves between leagues, it predicted a player's numbers in his new league about 8% better than his old numbers did, in 6 of the 7 skills (not dribbling). The amounts are modest, and players who move after a good season can make a lower league look a little easier than it is. Only the skill scores use it: a player's Ability allows for level through the strength of his clubs instead.

Skills count for less at a lower level

Judgement

Assumes: beyond the measured league differences above, a skill shown against weaker opponents is worth less. A player's skill scores are pulled down by how far his clubs' strength sits below the main leagues' average (0.75 standard deviations for every 100 points, about a goal a game); players at or above it are not lifted.

Why: the owner's judgement that a League Two winger's dribbling numbers shouldn't read as Premier League class, as Ability already starts from club strength.

Checked: not tested against results: players who moved up a league kept most of their numbers, so the data alone doesn't support this cut. It changes no rank, only the skill scores.

Positions are worth different amounts

Judgement

Assumes: being in the top few percent of strikers counts for more than being in the top few percent of full-backs, so stats count fully for strikers, wingers and attacking midfielders and less for midfielders and defenders.

Why: without it, a very good full-back ranked above a very good striker.

Checked: not testable with our data: the starting XIs' ratings add almost nothing to the club ratings when predicting results, so results can't say what a position is worth.

The less we have seen of a player, the lower we rate him

Judgement

Assumes: a player with few minutes is pulled towards a level below an average regular until he has played more, and only his last 20 appearances within 18 months count towards his current form.

Why: players who don't play usually aren't picked for a reason, and a few good games are mostly luck.

Checked: the amount (worth about 900 minutes) is a judgement.

Players improve, peak, then decline

Judgement

Assumes: players improve up to about 24, hold their level, then decline from 31 (33 for goalkeepers), faster each year. Teenagers get a set step up each year.

Why: this is the shape the data shows, and it lets a player with no minutes this season still have a rating.

Checked: the curve after the teenage years is measured from players' ratings in consecutive seasons; the teenage steps are set by hand. Neither has been tested against results.

A goalkeeper's stats say little

Tested on past matches

Assumes: a keeper's rating is mostly his club's level, with his stats moving him by up to about 10 points, and a keeper who hasn't played this season at a club that has is a backup and is marked down.

Why: save percentage barely repeats from one season to the next (0.18), so it is mostly luck.

Checked: how well each keeper stat repeats was measured; the split between club and stats is a judgement.

Ratings only where there is match-by-match data

Judgement

Assumes: players are rated only in the 13 leagues with match-by-match player data, always compared with players in the original 10, so adding a league doesn't move everyone else.

Why: a rating needs per-match stats and minutes. The ratings can't see what the data doesn't record: work off the ball, or a player carrying an injury.

Predicted line-ups

Who has been playing will play

Checked on live matches

Assumes: a club starts one goalkeeper and the ten outfield players with the most minutes over its last five matches, in the shape it has been using. For cup matches, minutes in the league count.

Why: recent minutes are the best guide we have before the team sheet is out.

Checked: every prediction saved before kick-off is scored against the official XI on the Line-up record. Managers rotate, and late news isn't always reported, so it will sometimes be wrong.

Anyone on the injury list doesn't start

Judgement

Assumes: every player listed as injured, suspended or doubtful is left out, and so is a player whose latest match was for the opponent.

Why: it is simpler and safer than guessing which doubts will clear. Doubtful players do sometimes play, so this is a known source of error.

Match predictions

Goals follow a Poisson pattern, with a boost for draws

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: once each side's expected goals are known, the number it scores follows a Poisson distribution (the usual pattern for rare events), separately for each side. Because that under-counts draws, the draw is raised by up to 10% in close games, fading to nothing at a 1.5-goal margin.

Why: it is the standard approach for football scores and needs only two numbers a match.

Checked: the chances it gives match how often things happen in the middle of the range; strong favourites win a little more often than it says. Sharpening the chances and refitting the draw boost helped slightly on past matches and are being scored on live matches; a model that lets the two sides' goals depend on each other has been tried too.

The last 12 months tell you how many goals a club scores and concedes

Tested on past matches

Assumes: each side's usual goals for and against, at home or away as the match is, over the last 12 months (xG where a match has it), pulled towards the competition's average as if it had played six more average games; and mostly (75%) each club's attack and defence ratings for how open a game will be.

Why: a short record is mostly luck, so it is pulled towards the average.

Checked: the attack and defence part improved every market on past matches.

A missing player matters by how much he plays

Tested on past matches

Assumes: each player listed out or doubtful counts by his share of his club's minutes over its last ten matches; one ever-present player missing is worth 0.1 goals. Only in the 10 leagues with injury history.

Why: a regular's absence matters more than a squad player's. A long-term absentee has no recent minutes, so he already isn't in the side's recent results.

Checked: trained on 2021/22 to 2023/24 and tested on 2024/25 onwards, it helped a little, but consistently. Weighting by rating or by position made no real difference.

The predicted XIs matter a little

Tested on past matches

Assumes: the gap between the two predicted XIs' ratings in defence, midfield and attack moves the expected margin slightly (0.005 goals per point in each line). The goalkeeper is left out.

Why: much of what a line-up tells you is already in the club's rating.

Checked: it helped a little on past matches, and using the real team sheets instead helped only slightly more. Counting the goalkeeper made predictions worse.

The same rules everywhere

Tested on past matches Checked on live matches

Assumes: the same settings work in every league and cup we cover, apart from the European home bonus.

Why: most competitions have too few matches to set their own.

Checked: accuracy is shown competition by competition on the Stats page. Widening the gaps between leagues in cup ties helped in one period and hurt in another, so it isn't used.

Bookmakers' odds are not an input

Tested on past matches

Assumes: the model is built from football data alone.

Why: so its record says how good its own reading of the game is. The bookmakers are more accurate so far; blending their odds in hasn't yet helped on the matches available, and will be tested again with more.

A change is made only when it is proven better

Checked on live matches

Assumes: the settings above stay fixed until a change is shown to be better on matches it wasn't tuned on, with how it would be judged written down before the result was seen. A challenger, the same model with boosted trees correcting its past errors, runs beside it on every match, and both are recorded.

Checked: every change, with its tests, is on The Model.

What none of the models can see

  • Team news that hasn't been reported: illness, rotation, a player carrying a knock.
  • Tactics, motivation (a dead rubber, a cup run), a new manager's first weeks.
  • Weather, pitch, travel and referees.
  • Anything the data source doesn't record, or records late or wrongly.

If you think an assumption here is wrong, or one is missing, please tell us.
